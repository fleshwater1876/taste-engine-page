Abstract

Music apps recommend from what millions of other people listen to, not from what you hear, so a listener with no history gets popular songs instead of personal ones. With one listener and a small library there is no other people's data to use at all.

I built a taste engine that compares songs by sound: every track becomes a 512-number vector with CLAP, your 1–5★ ratings become one taste profile, and the five closest unrated songs come back with the liked song behind each pick. The catalogue has 376 songs: my 76 songs and 300 possible new picks in 19 style labels.

I tested two versions with three friends each. v1, with picks from all styles, found 6 of 9 hidden favourites; random picks from 293 unseen songs would find about 0.15. v2, with picks limited to the friend's styles, found 3 of 9, against 1.77 expected from random picks inside those styles. The groups were different, so this is not a direct v1-versus-v2 comparison.

How it works: sound → numbers → five picks

CLAP turns a 30-second clip into 512 numbers; your ratings turn those numbers into one taste.

01

Listen

A 30-second clip of each song becomes 512 numbers with CLAP. Songs that sound alike land close together.

02

Your taste

The 4–5★ songs are averaged, and the 1–2★ songs are subtracted, to make one taste vector.

03

New songs

The five unrated library songs closest to that vector come back, each naming the liked song it is closest to.

How we test it

Every number has a baseline, so a hit is more than a lucky pick.

Reading the results

Only three friends tried each version, with 10 ratings each. v1 and v2 used different people, so 6 of 9 and 3 of 9 cannot be compared directly. The library has 300 candidates, the clips are 30 seconds, and the style labels are Apple search words.

This is one listener's taste in one small library, not proof for a general audience. v2 beats the same-style random baseline only 1.7 times, so “sound beats style” is not a safe claim yet.
